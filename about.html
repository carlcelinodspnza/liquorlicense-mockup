Who decides

One agency stands behind every licence in this state

Before anything about this firm is worth explaining, it is worth being precise about where the authority sits. It does not sit with us.

The regulator

The California Department of Alcoholic Beverage Control

Licensing and compliance in California are governed by the California Department of Alcoholic Beverage Control, shortened by everyone in the trade to the ABC. It is one state agency holding both halves of the job: it decides who may hold a liquor licence, and it decides whether a licence already held is being used within its terms.

That combination is why licence work does not behave like an ordinary sale of an asset. A private agreement between a buyer and a seller settles a price. It does not settle the outcome. Until the ABC has the file it needs, and is satisfied with what is in it, nothing agreed between the parties is final, and commercial goodwill on both sides is no substitute for that.

  • It regulates the sale of alcoholic beverages across the whole of California
  • It is the licensing authority, so the application is made to it and only it can grant
  • It is also the compliance authority, so the body that grants a licence is the body that supervises it afterwards

We are brokers rather than the regulator, and we say so plainly because the distinction is the thing a client is really buying. What a brokerage controls is the quality, the completeness and the order of what reaches the ABC, and how little of the waiting is spent on avoidable questions. The order those approvals actually arrive in, and the window they typically take, belong to the transfer process page rather than to this one.

How the firm is built

Two disciplines, kept on one desk

A California licence transaction is really two jobs running at once. Most firms are good at one of them. This one was built to hold both, because the file only closes when both are finished.

The transaction desk

This is the commercial half, and it runs on knowledge of a market rather than knowledge of a rulebook: who holds what, which holders would move for the right number, and what comparable licences have settled at rather than what they were advertised at. Its output is an agreement two parties are still standing behind three months later.

Deals in this trade are seldom lost on price. They are lost on time, on a party who did not understand what they had committed to, or on a piece of information that surfaced far too late to be worked with. Somebody has to own the middle of a transaction, and that ownership is what this half of the firm exists to supply.

The regulatory desk

This is the half that speaks to authority, and its raw material is what a regulator will and will not accept: how a file is assembled, what a condition attached to a licence obliges an operator to do, and where an application meets a standard rather than a preference. None of it is negotiable in the commercial sense. A regulator is not persuaded. It is satisfied, or it is not.

It is slower work than a negotiation and far less visible, and it is where transactions quietly stall. A file that is complete on the day it is lodged moves. A file with a gap in it waits, and waiting is the expensive part of this business, because everything an operator has scheduled around an approval date is still running while the answer is outstanding.

Split those two disciplines across two firms and the seam between them becomes the client's problem. The broker who agreed the terms is not the person who has to defend them in a filing; the consultant preparing the filing was not in the room when the terms were agreed. Both are working honestly and neither holds the whole picture. Keeping them on one desk means the commercial decision is taken by people who already know what the regulator will do with it, and the filing is prepared by people who were in the negotiation. One file, one history, one set of people answering for it.

1,000+ Licences brokered Licences taken through a completed transaction, not enquiries answered or listings held. For an operator this is a once-in-a-career event; at this volume it is ordinary work, which is the whole difference between a process explained to you and a process run for you.
