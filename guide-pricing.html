Knowledge base › What a licence costs

Price is set by scarcity, and scarcity is local

Nothing about what a licence sells for reflects the cost of producing it. The document itself is administrative. Its value is a function of how many of them the state permits to exist in the county where you want one.

California ties the number of general licences a county may hold to that county’s population — not to the number of businesses that would like one. Those two figures move at different speeds and are not connected. A county whose hospitality trade grows faster than its residential population does not receive extra licences to match; it simply runs out. From that point the only licences available there are the ones already issued, and every operator who wants to open in that county is drawing from the same closed pool.

That is why a price is a local number rather than a state one. Two counties can be an hour apart and value the same classification at levels that have nothing to say to each other, because the ratio of demand to allocation is different on each side of the line. It is also why a price is a snapshot rather than a constant: it shifts as businesses open, close and relocate inside a county, largely independently of what is happening elsewhere in California. Establishing what one named licence is worth in one named county on one named day is a separate exercise — see licence valuation — and what a buyer should expect to budget on top of the purchase itself is answered in the FAQs.
