The transfer process

From consultation to ABC approval: the six phases of a licence transfer

A California licence changes hands in a fixed order. Knowing which phase you are in — and whose decision the transaction is waiting on next — is what turns a rough estimate into a date you can plan an opening around.

Talk to a broker See the six phases

Phase by phase

Six phases, in the order they actually happen

They run in this order, and what changes across them is who the transaction is waiting on. The early phases are yours to pace. The last ones belong to the offices reviewing the file.

Before phase 01

Two kinds of transfer, one sequence

California transfers carry two names — person-to-person and premises-to-premises. What each one involves is set out under the transfer service, and whether a licence can be moved to a new address is answered on the FAQ page. What they share is the sequence: whichever kind you are doing, the six phases below run in the same order, and the phase you are in is what tells you whose decision the transaction is waiting on.

  • Both kinds run through the same six phases, in the same order
  • The phase you are in names the decision that is outstanding
  • Who makes that decision changes as the transaction moves
What a transfer involves Moving a licence to a new address
  • Phase 01

    The requirement is defined

    The county, the concept and the licence type are settled before anything else moves — 47, 48, 21 or 20 — because every later phase is scoped by that one answer. Why the classification decision carries the weight it does is set out in the knowledge base.

    Why classification decides so much
  • Phase 02

    Available licences are identified

    A shortlist is built from what genuinely exists in that county, at that classification, right now. What is on the board today — each licence with its county, its asking price and its transfer status — sits on the inventory page.

    Licences on the board today
  • Phase 03

    Eligibility is checked — on the licence and on the address

    Two questions are asked at the same time, and neither is a formality. Is this licence transferable? And will the local authority allow it here? Most transfers require city or county approval, often including zoning clearance and sometimes a Conditional Use Permit — which is why permit and hearing work is handled as a discipline of its own.

    Conditional Use Permits and hearings Why an address is judged separately
  • Phase 04

    Price and terms are agreed

    Terms are negotiated and the Purchase Agreement is coordinated around them. This is the last phase in which the two parties still set the pace between themselves. What a licence costs, and what sits on top of the purchase price, is answered on the FAQ page.

    What a licence costs
  • Phase 05

    Statutory escrow opens

    California law requires specific escrow procedures on a licence transfer to protect creditors, with statutory notices filed under the Business and Professions Code. Escrow here is not a convenience the buyer and seller can agree to skip between themselves — it is part of what makes the transfer lawful, and the notices are filed whether or not anyone asks for them. Who runs that escrow, and what the coordinator does day to day, is a separate question.

    Who runs the escrow
  • Phase 06

    The application is carried to ABC approval

    A California liquor-licence transfer typically takes 60 to 120 days. Four things move that number: background checks, application completeness, local approvals and whether protests are filed. Only one of the four — completeness — sits with the applicant, which makes it the one place a transfer can buy back time. The licence is not yours until the Department of Alcoholic Beverage Control issues it in your name.

Who has to say yes

Who signs off, and when

Four parties shape how a California transfer moves. They do not all decide, they do not all act at the same point, and none of them work backwards from your opening date.

Two decisions, not one

The state decides on the licence; the city or county decides on the address

Those two decisions are made separately, against different criteria, by offices that are not comparing calendars. Both have to land before a transfer completes, and neither one substitutes for the other. It is why the two most useful questions at any point in a transfer are which phase you are in, and which of the two decisions is still open.

  • State side — the ABC, on the transfer application
  • Local side — the city or county, on the address
  • Both must land; neither stands in for the other
